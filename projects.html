Case Study 01

Sales and Customer Insights Analysis for a Family Retail Business

Turning twelve months of shop floor invoices into clear stocking rules, staffing schedules, and promotion plans using Microsoft Excel and Power BI.

Project Type Retail Business Intelligence
Duration 6 Weeks
My Role Solo Data and Business Analyst
Period Covered January to December 2025
  • Microsoft Excel
  • Power Query
  • Power BI
  • Pivot Tables
  • DAX Measures

Project Overview

Running a family retail shop means making daily choices about what to restock, how much cash to hold for fast moving goods, and when to run discounts. For years, those decisions inside our family store relied on memory and manual receipt books. While experienced staff knew which shelves emptied fastest, nobody had a clear twelve month picture of seasonal peaks, slow moving stock, or customer basket sizes.

I compiled a full year of retail sales logs into a clean Microsoft Excel model and built an interactive Power BI dashboard for weekly review. The goal was to replace rough estimates with measurable facts that directly guide purchasing orders, shelf placement, and weekend staffing.

The Business Problem

Cash was repeatedly tied up in slow selling gift packs while high demand pantry staples ran out of stock on busy Saturdays. In addition, discounts were offered across random weeks without checking whether lower prices actually brought more total revenue or simply reduced profit margin on items customers already bought at full price.

Project Objectives

  1. Measure monthly revenue and order volume trends across all twelve calendar months to spot seasonal cycles.
  2. Rank product categories and individual stock keeping units from highest to lowest contribution to total revenue and margin.
  3. Identify peak trading days and hours so shelf restocking and counter staffing match real customer footfall.
  4. Examine customer purchase behaviour, including average basket value and repeat buying across product categories.
  5. Deliver practical stocking and promotional recommendations that free up working capital and protect store margins.

Data Used and Cleaning Process

The dataset came from daily point of sale receipts and handwritten ledger entries exported to comma separated files. It contained 4,820 transaction rows covering January 1, 2025 to December 31, 2025 across 64 active products.

Field Name Data Type Description
Transaction_ID Text Unique receipt reference assigned at checkout
Sale_Date Date Calendar date and weekday used for trend grouping
Category Text Product group such as Pantry, Beverages, Cleaning, or Gift Packs
Unit_Price Currency Selling price per unit in Nigerian Naira
Quantity_Sold Whole Number Units purchased on that invoice line
Customer_Type Text Walk in buyer or registered repeat household account

Before building any charts, I cleaned the workbook in Power Query and Excel:

  • Missing values: Filled 38 blank category labels by matching product names against the master supplier catalogue, and flagged 11 invoices missing quantity values by checking paper duplicates.
  • Duplicates: Removed 24 exact duplicate rows caused by double scanning during busy weekend shifts.
  • Formatting: Standardised mixed text dates into ISO dates, trimmed trailing spaces on product names, and converted currency strings into numeric Naira values.
  • Outliers: Separated 14 bulk wholesale orders from regular retail receipts into a separate flag so large one off invoices did not distort typical daily customer basket averages.

Method and Process

  1. Ledger consolidation: Merged twelve monthly receipt sheets into one structured Excel table with calculated columns for line revenue and weekday name.
  2. Exploratory Pivot Tables: Tested revenue by month, category share, and weekday volume inside Excel to verify totals against cashbook summaries.
  3. Star schema modelling: Loaded the cleaned sales table, product lookup table, and a continuous calendar table into Power BI Desktop.
  4. DAX metric creation: Wrote clear measures for total revenue, average basket value, month over month growth, and stock turn contribution.
  5. Dashboard design and review: Built a three view report for monthly trends, product ranking, and trading day patterns, then walked through the findings with family store managers.

Key Findings

34%

Quarter four surge: October to December generated 34% of annual turnover, with December alone beating February sales by 58%. Meaning: supplier orders must scale up by late September.

64%

Top two categories: Staple Pantry Goods (38%) and Beverages and Dairy (26%) drove 64% of store revenue. Meaning: these shelves must never suffer stockouts.

5%

Weakest product line: Specialty Gift Packs made up only 5% of revenue while sitting unsold for an average of 47 days. Meaning: heavy cash was locked in slow shelves.

41%

Weekend peak trading: Friday and Saturday delivered 41% of weekly revenue, with Saturday baskets 28% larger than Tuesday orders. Meaning: schedule full staff on Fridays and Saturdays.

2.7x

Registered repeat buyers: Repeat household accounts spent 2.7 times more per year than walk in buyers and accounted for 52% of pantry orders. Meaning: retention protects core revenue.

Click any chart to open a larger view.

Results and Recommendations

  1. Reallocate restocking capital: Cut reorders on Specialty Gift Packs by 50% outside December and move that budget into Staple Pantry Goods and Beverages, expected to reduce weekly stockouts by over 30%.
  2. Shift promotion timing to midweek: Run bundle offers on Tuesdays and Wednesdays rather than discounting on Saturdays when store traffic is already strong, protecting weekend margins while lifting slow midweek turnover by an estimated 12% to 15%.
  3. Align restocking and counter shifts: Complete shelf replenishment every Thursday afternoon ahead of the Friday and Saturday rush so customers find full stock on peak days.

Challenges and How I Handled Them

Early paper invoices often wrote informal abbreviations for the same product, such as "Peak Tin" and "Peak Milk 160g", which initially split one item into multiple rows inside Pivot Tables. I created a mapping table in Excel that matched every shorthand spelling to one official product name before loading the records into Power BI.

What I Learned

Working on the shop floor taught me what customers complain about, while Excel and Power BI showed me the exact cost of those gaps in Naira. Combining my marketing background with structured data cleaning proved that simple operational metrics can immediately improve cash flow without expensive software.

Case Study 02

Customer Segmentation and Repeat Purchase Analysis

Grouping retail customers by recency, frequency, and monetary value in Python to measure typical reorder timing and plan targeted marketing outreach.

Project Type Marketing Analytics in Python
Duration 4 Weeks
My Role Data Science Student Analyst
Period Covered 18 Months of Customer Orders
  • Python
  • pandas
  • matplotlib
  • RFM Scoring
  • Jupyter Notebook

Project Overview

Sending the same promotional message to every buyer on a contact list wastes time and annoys regular customers with offers meant for lapsed shoppers. As part of my Data Science coursework and marketing practice, I wanted to group customers by how recently they bought, how often they return, and how much they spend overall.

Using Python with pandas and matplotlib, I built a reproducible segmentation notebook that scores each customer, measures the median days between repeat purchases, and maps out specific retention actions for loyal, regular, at risk, and occasional buyers.

The Business Problem

The business knew several long time customers had stopped coming back, yet had no way to notice a quiet drop off until months had passed. Without knowing how many days typically separate one order from the next, staff could not tell the difference between a normal pause and a customer who was genuinely at risk of leaving for a competitor.

Project Objectives

  1. Calculate Recency, Frequency, and Monetary (RFM) scores for every identified customer account in pandas.
  2. Group buyers into clear behavioural segments: Loyal Customers, Regular Buyers, At Risk Group, and Occasional Buyers.
  3. Measure the typical time gap between repeat purchases using the median rather than the arithmetic mean.
  4. Quantify each segment's share of total customer count and share of total store revenue.
  5. Design practical, respectful marketing follow up actions tailored to each customer tier.

Data Used and Cleaning Process

The dataset consisted of 6,140 customer linked order rows spanning July 2024 to December 2025 across 410 unique customer IDs.

Column Data Type Purpose in Analysis
Customer_ID String Groups all orders placed by the same household or buyer
Order_Date Datetime Computes days since last order (Recency) and gaps between visits
Invoice_No String Counts distinct shopping trips (Frequency)
Order_Total Float Sums lifetime spend over the 18 month window (Monetary)
Channel String Tracks walk in versus WhatsApp order placement

Data cleaning steps implemented in pandas:

  • Missing values: Filtered out 312 anonymous walk in receipts that carried no Customer_ID, since repeat gaps cannot be tracked without an identifier.
  • Duplicates: Deduplicated repeated invoice rows using drop_duplicates() on Invoice_No and Order_Total.
  • Formatting: Parsed dates with pd.to_datetime() and normalised phone based customer keys to remove leading whitespace.
  • Outliers and cancelled orders: Removed 19 voided negative invoices and checked extreme spenders so quartile thresholds stayed balanced.

Method and Process

  1. Customer aggregation: Grouped transactions by Customer_ID in pandas to compute days since last purchase, count of unique invoices, and sum of spend.
  2. Quartile scoring: Ranked Recency, Frequency, and Monetary metrics from 1 to 4 using pd.qcut(), reversing Recency so more recent buyers scored higher.
  3. Segment assignment: Combined RFM scores into four practical groups that a store team can act on without technical jargon.
  4. Reorder interval analysis: Sorted each repeat buyer's order dates chronologically, computed day differences with .diff().dt.days, and calculated the median gap.
  5. Why median instead of mean: I chose the median reorder gap (19 days) instead of the arithmetic mean (31 days) because purchase intervals are right skewed. A small number of customers who paused for 120 or 180 days pull the mean upward, making customers appear slower to return than they truly are. The median shows the exact midpoint of real repeat shoppers.

Key Findings

19 Days

Median reorder gap: Half of all repeat buyers returned within 19 days, compared to a skewed mean of 31 days. Meaning: reminder outreach works best around day 18 to day 24.

51%

Loyal Customers share: Just 22% of identified buyers (90 customers) generated 51% of tracked revenue with a median gap of 12 days. Meaning: keeping these 90 households satisfied protects half of repeat income.

18%

At Risk Customers: 18% of customers (74 buyers) previously ordered three or more times but have not returned in over 45 days. Meaning: they represent high value accounts worth recovering.

34%

Regular Buyers: 34% of buyers accounted for 28% of revenue on a 24 day median cycle. Meaning: gentle bundle nudges can lift their basket size into the loyal tier.

26%

Occasional Buyers: 26% of buyers purchased once or twice and generated only 7% of revenue. Meaning: broad low cost awareness suits them better than heavy discounts.

Visuals and Matplotlib Outputs

Click any chart to enlarge it inside the viewer.

Targeted Actions and Expected Impact

  1. Loyal Customers (22% of buyers): Send early WhatsApp stock alerts when fresh staple batches arrive and offer free local delivery above a threshold rather than cutting prices. Expected impact: higher retention with zero margin erosion.
  2. At Risk Group (18% of buyers): Trigger a polite personal check in message at day 40 with a modest return voucher valid for ten days. Expected impact: recovering 20% of lapsed buyers restores meaningful monthly turnover.
  3. Regular Buyers (34% of buyers): Share curated fortnightly household bundles timed to the 19 day median reorder window to encourage consistent basket growth.
  4. Occasional Buyers (26% of buyers): Capture preferred contact consent at checkout and send seasonal catalogue updates once per month without heavy manual follow up.

Challenges and How I Handled Them

When testing my first Python script, customers with only one order produced null values for their reorder gap, which caused an error inside the plotting function. I separated single purchase customers before running .diff() on repeat accounts and verified row counts at every merge step so no buyer disappeared silently.

What I Learned

In marketing, people often talk about "VIP customers" and "lost leads" in vague terms. Writing RFM logic in Python turned those ideas into clear rules grounded in real numbers, and taught me why choosing the right summary measure such as the median protects a business from acting on distorted averages.
